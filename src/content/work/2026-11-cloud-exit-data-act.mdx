Brussels spent November arguing about which cloud Europe should buy. On
11 November two members of the European Parliament presented their draft
report on the Cloud and AI Development Act, the proposal the Commission
tabled on 3 June. Committees discuss it on 1 December and vote in 2027
([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/11/eu-lawmakers-push-to-reduce-europes-dependence-on-foreign-cloud-providers),
[Parliament procedure file](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/procedure-file?reference=2026%2F0138%28COD%29)).
That law is years from touching a 40-person company.

A quieter rule lands much sooner, and it is already law. On 12 January 2027
the last piece of the EU Data Act's switching chapter switches on. If you rent
a data platform from anyone, it changes one line of your exit bill. Only one.

## What changes on 12 January 2027?

Your provider may no longer charge you for switching away. Article 29 of the
[Data Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/2854/oj) says that from
12 January 2027 providers of data processing services "shall not impose any
switching charges on the customer for the switching process". Until that day
they may still charge, but only their own direct costs of the switch
([Bundesnetzagentur glossary](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/SharedDocs/A_Z_Glossar_DataAct_ENG/S/switching-charge.html)).

"Data processing service" is the law's name for cloud: infrastructure,
platforms and software you rent. A Fabric capacity, a BigQuery project and
the SaaS tool your finance team lives in all count. The rules apply to
customers in the EU whatever the provider's home country.

## Does that include egress fees?

Yes, for the switch itself. Egress is the per-gigabyte fee for moving data
out of a cloud. The Data Act's definition of switching charges names data
egress charges explicitly, and the Commission has confirmed that reading
([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/law-in-tech/2026/cloud-exit-under-the-eu-data-act)).

It does not make all egress free. If you keep both clouds and move data
between them every night, that is "in-parallel use". From the same date the
provider may still bill egress for it, but only to pass on its own costs
([Travers Smith](https://www.traverssmith.com/knowledge/knowledge-container/the-data-acts-changes-to-cloud-services-contracts-tipping-the-scales-on-switching/)).
Cheaper, not zero.

## How long can a provider make you wait?

About three months in the normal case. These parts have applied since
12 September 2025
([Bundesnetzagentur](https://bundesnetzagentur.de/EN/Areas/Digitalisation/DataAct/background/3-provider/start.html)):

- **Notice:** two months at most.
- **Transition:** 30 calendar days after the notice runs out, during which
  the provider has to help. You may extend it once. The provider may stretch
  it to seven months if the move is technically not feasible in time, but it
  has to tell you so within 14 working days and justify it.
- **Retrieval:** at least 30 more days to pull out anything left.

Your contract has to spell this out. If yours predates September 2025 and
says nothing about switching, ask for the current terms.

## What does the law not make free?

Three things, and they are the big ones.

**The rest of your contract.** The definition of a switching charge excludes
standard service fees and early termination penalties. If you signed a
three-year commitment for a discount, leaving in year two can still cost you,
as long as the penalty is proportionate and you were told before signing.

**Running two platforms at once.** No real migration is a clean cut. For a
few weeks you pay the old platform and the new one, plus cost-based egress
between them.

**The work.** This is the one that decides whether you can actually leave.
The Data Act obliges a provider to hand over your exportable data in a
structured, commonly used, machine-readable format. It does not oblige
anyone to rebuild what you built on top.

## If the fee was the small part, what is the lock-in?

Everything that only runs on one vendor's platform. Your tables leave. Your
scheduled pipelines, SQL written in one dialect, semantic models, report
layouts, access rules and alerts do not. They get rebuilt by people, and
people cost more than gigabytes.

The fee was already fading before the law caught up. Google has waived
transfer fees for customers who leave since January 2024, and Microsoft and
AWS followed that spring
([UK CMA overview](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/688b8169fc784fa12a089071/Appendix_N_-_Egress_fees___free_switching_programmes.pdf)).
Read the conditions, though.
[Google's programme](https://cloud.google.com/exit-cloud) asks for an exit
notice, gives you 30 days to start and at least 30 more to finish, covers
only a move of all your workloads on that service, and pays the fee back as
a credit on your final invoice. From January, for EU customers, the zero
fee is a legal right, no longer a programme you apply for.

So the exit bill for a small data team looks like this: transfer fee, zero.
Engineering weeks, the same as before.

## How do you keep your own exit cheap?

By deciding where your logic lives before you need to move it. Five habits
do most of the work.

1. **Keep tables in an open format.** OneLake stores tables as Delta
   Parquet or Iceberg, both open standards other engines can read
   ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/fabric/onelake/onelake-overview)).
   BigQuery exports tables to Cloud Storage with no processing charge
   ([Google](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/pricing)). Use that. Avoid
   storage only one tool can open.
2. **Keep transformations in code you own.** SQL models in a Git
   repository move with you. Logic clicked together inside a vendor's
   visual designer mostly does not.
3. **Write your definitions down outside the BI tool.** If "active
   customer" only exists inside one report, it has to be dug out again.
4. **Know your contract dates.** The commitment end date and any early
   termination clause set your cheapest moment to leave.
5. **Do one test export.** Take a real table out and open it somewhere
   else. An hour now tells you more than the contract does.

I build on both Fabric and BigQuery, and neither is a trap by default. What
traps a team is building it so that nobody could say what leaving would
involve. [Fabric or BigQuery for a small European team](/work/2026-09-fabric-vs-bigquery)
compares the two, and [an EU region is not sovereignty](/work/2026-09-eu-region-not-sovereignty)
covers the legal side of the same choice.

## Could the rules still change before January?

Not the charge ban, as things stand. The Commission's Digital Omnibus would
exempt some older contracts for custom-made services, and for services from
smaller providers, from most of the switching chapter. Even that proposal
keeps the duty to reduce and then remove switching and egress charges
([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/denmark/the-digital-omnibus-on-switching-between-data-processing-services-under-the-data-act--whats-new)).
It is still being negotiated and is not law. I will update this post if
that changes.
