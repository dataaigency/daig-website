Most pages that compare Microsoft Fabric with Power BI compare features. Both
have dashboards, both connect to lots of sources, both have a Copilot button.
Then they conclude that Fabric is "more powerful", which is true of a lorry
compared to a bicycle and tells you nothing about your commute.

So here is the answer the listicles avoid. If you are a team of five to
twenty people with a handful of business systems, you probably do not need
Fabric yet. One database and Power BI Pro will carry you further than the
sales material suggests. And there are three concrete signals that tell you
when that stops being true.

## What is the "just Power BI and a database" stack?

It is the boring version. Your business apps (CRM, webshop, finance tool) load
their data into one database every night. A plain Postgres or SQL database in a
European region is fine. Power BI reads it, imports it on a schedule, and your
people open the reports.

No lakehouse, no capacity to pause, no medallion layers. If you have read my
posts on [the warehouse or lakehouse question](/work/2026-08-warehouse-or-lakehouse)
or [the medallion mistakes](/work/2026-08-medallion-mistakes), this is the
stage before all that. It is a perfectly respectable place to live.

## What does it cost, compared with Fabric?

The database is the small part. The smallest burstable PostgreSQL server in
West Europe lists at roughly €13 a month on third-party price trackers, a dollar list
price converted at the 1.1664 ECB reference rate of 25 August 2026
([Bytebase's Azure price table](https://www.bytebase.com/dbcost/azure-flexible/instance/B1ms.md)).
That is only the entry tier, and I could not check Microsoft's own euro page
when writing, so open the Azure pricing page, pick West Europe and
euros, and read today's number before you plan around it.

The seats are the real line. Power BI Pro runs about €13 per user per month in
Europe, based on Microsoft's 14 dollar list price after the April 2025
increase ([Toucan Toco's European breakdown](https://www.toucantoco.com/en/blog/power-bi-pricing)).
Twenty readers is about €260 a month.

Now compare. A pay as you go Fabric F2 left on around the clock is about €278
a month, around €165 on a one year reservation, and on every capacity below F64
your readers still need their own Pro seat on top
([my F2 pricing breakdown has the sources](/work/2026-08-fabric-f2-pricing)).
So Fabric at this size does not replace the plain stack's cost. It adds to it.
That extra spend has to buy you something. The next three sections are what it
should buy.

## When does Power BI Pro alone run out? Tripwire one: the model gets too big

A Power BI Pro semantic model is capped at 1 GB. Premium Per User raises the
ceiling to 100 GB, which Microsoft describes as an upper bound rather than a
promise ([Microsoft's Premium Per User FAQ](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/fabric/enterprise/powerbi/service-premium-per-user-faq)).

One gigabyte sounds tiny, but Power BI compresses imported data hard. For a
company with a few years of invoices, orders and web analytics, it is often
enough. It stops being enough with event level data, such as every click or
every sensor reading, which grows quickly. If your reports are heading there,
that is a real reason to look at something built for volume.

## Tripwire two: you need fresher data than eight refreshes a day

On Pro, a model can refresh on a schedule up to eight times a day. Premium Per
User and capacities allow 48
([Microsoft community answers confirm the Pro limit](https://community.fabric.microsoft.com/t5/Service/Limit-Refresh-Power-BI-Semantic-Model-Using-Power-Automate/m-p/4741699)).

Eight is plenty for a sales review that is read each morning. It is not enough
if the warehouse team wants stock levels every fifteen minutes. Be honest about
whether anyone actually acts on data that fresh. In my experience most "we need
real time" requests turn out to mean "we need it by 9am", which one night
refresh handles for free.

## Tripwire three: nobody owns the sources

This is the one that has nothing to do with software limits, and it is the most
common reason I tell teams to start building structure.

When your numbers come from nine places and three of them are spreadsheets
someone emails around, the database stops being a tidy hub and becomes another
copy. Revenue means one thing in the CRM and another in finance. No tool fixes
that, and a bigger platform just moves the argument into a lakehouse.

What helps is a named owner per source and a simple agreed definition per
metric, written down. If you reach the point where that agreement needs
layers, history and checks that run themselves, that is when a lakehouse
earns its place. [My lakehouse architecture work](/services/lakehouse-architecture)
is exactly that step.

## What if I just want to be ready?

Resist it. Buying a platform for the company you hope to be in three years
means paying the floor price today for capacity that sits idle. Fabric is
rented by the hour whether it is busy or not, and a small team rarely uses
what it rented.

You can also move later without a rewrite. Your data model, your metric
definitions and the habit of having an owner for each source all travel with
you. The expensive mistake is not starting small. It is starting big with the
sources still unowned.

## So which one should you pick?

Start with one European region database and Power BI Pro seats for the people
who read and build. Write down who owns each source. Then watch for the three
tripwires: a model near 1 GB, a real need for more than eight refreshes a day,
or sources nobody can vouch for.

If none of them has tripped, spend your money on something else. If one has,
Fabric starts to make sense, and the sizing advice in the
[Fabric or BigQuery comparison](/work/2026-09-fabric-vs-bigquery) applies.
