Most BigQuery cost guides say the same thing: once you scan enough data, move
to reservations. Then they quote a threshold in dollars, for US regions, and
leave you to guess whether it holds for a project in Belgium or the
Netherlands. It doesn't, quite. And the threshold is the wrong question
anyway. Volume doesn't decide which model is cheaper. The shape of your
queries does.

## What are you actually paying for in each model?

On-demand bills the bytes your queries scan. Capacity pricing, which Google
sells as BigQuery editions, bills slot time. Slots are BigQuery's virtual
CPUs, and you pay for them by the slot-hour.

So the same query runs through two different meters. A query that reads a
wide table but only filters and sums it scans a lot and computes very
little. A query that reads a modest table but joins it five ways, sorts it
and runs window functions over it scans little and computes a lot. The first
kind is cheaper on slots. The second kind is cheaper on-demand. Most
workloads are a mix, which is why nobody can hand you a universal threshold.

Here are the European list prices. Google publishes them in USD, so I
converted at the ECB reference rate of 1.1052 dollars per euro (13 November
2026). Prices are from
[Google's BigQuery pricing page](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/pricing),
checked 16 November 2026. If your billing account pays in euros, Google
charges the EUR prices listed on its
[Cloud Platform SKUs page](https://cloud.google.com/skus/?currency=EUR&filter=BigQuery),
so check your exact line there.

| Location | On-demand, per TiB | Standard, per slot-hour | Enterprise, per slot-hour | Break-even (Standard / Enterprise) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EU multi-region | €5.66 | €0.040 | €0.060 | 142 / 95 |
| Belgium, Netherlands (`europe-west1`, `europe-west4`) | €6.79 | €0.040 | €0.060 | 170 / 114 |
| Frankfurt (`europe-west3`) | €7.35 | €0.047 | €0.071 | 156 / 104 |
| Paris (`europe-west9`) | €7.07 | €0.047 | €0.071 | 150 / 100 |
| Madrid (`europe-southwest1`) | €7.07 | €0.043 | €0.064 | 166 / 111 |

The first TiB scanned each month is free on-demand, once per billing account.

One quirk worth knowing. The EU multi-region charges less per TiB than any
single European region, the same as the US. But slot prices in the EU
multi-region and in Belgium or the Netherlands are identical. So the same
workload crosses over to slots sooner in `europe-west4` than it does in the
EU multi-region.

## Where does the line actually fall?

Divide the on-demand price per TiB by the slot-hour price. That gives you how
many slot-hours the price of one TiB buys. In the EU multi-region on Standard
edition, €5.66 buys about 142 slot-hours.

That ratio is the whole decision. If your queries burn fewer than about 142
slot-hours for every TiB they scan, slots are cheaper. If they burn more,
on-demand is cheaper. The exchange rate cancels out of the division, so the
break-even holds whatever the euro does this month.

## Why does a slot bill come out higher than the slot time you used?

Because the autoscaler bills what it allocates, not what you use. It scales
in multiples of 50 slots, and by default each scale-up is billed for at
least one minute
([Google's autoscaling docs](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/slots-autoscaling-intro)).
A two-second dashboard query that triggers a scale-up still opens a 50-slot,
one-minute window. That's 50 slot-minutes, about €0.03 at EU Standard rates,
for a query that needed a fraction of it.

One of those is nothing. A dashboard firing small queries all day is a
different story, and that's exactly the workload small teams tend to have.
Google now offers fluid scaling, an opt-in setting per reservation that
bills per second with no minimum duration. If your traffic is bursty, it is
the first thing to switch on.

So treat 142 as a ceiling, not a target. If your number comes out at 130, the
rounding can eat the difference. I'd want real headroom below the break-even
before moving anything.

## How do you find your own number?

You already have it. BigQuery logs the slot time and bytes billed for every
job, including on-demand ones. Run this in the region where your data lives
(swap `region-eu` for `region-europe-west4` and so on):

```sql
SELECT
  SUM(total_bytes_billed) / POW(1024, 4) AS tib_billed,
  SUM(total_slot_ms) / 1000 / 3600 AS slot_hours,
  SAFE_DIVIDE(
    SUM(total_slot_ms) / 1000 / 3600,
    SUM(total_bytes_billed) / POW(1024, 4)
  ) AS slot_hours_per_tib
FROM `region-eu`.INFORMATION_SCHEMA.JOBS
WHERE creation_time > TIMESTAMP_SUB(CURRENT_TIMESTAMP(), INTERVAL 30 DAY)
  AND job_type = 'QUERY'
  AND statement_type != 'SCRIPT';
```

The last line matters. A multi-statement script creates a parent job whose
totals already include its children, so leaving it in counts everything
twice
([Google's INFORMATION_SCHEMA guidance](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/information-schema-jobs)).
Bytes billed already reflects what on-demand charges you: cached results are
free and small tables are rounded up to the 10 MB minimum.

Compare `slot_hours_per_tib` with the break-even for your location and
edition. Editions and on-demand can run side by side per project, so if the
number looks good you can test a reservation on one project before moving the
rest.

## Does any of this matter at small-team volume?

Often not, and that's worth saying out loud. Take a hypothetical team
scanning 3 TiB a month in the EU multi-region. The first TiB is free, so
on-demand costs 2 × €5.66, about €11 a month. No reservation saves
meaningful money on €11. Spend the time on partitioning instead.

It starts to matter around tens of TiB. Say the same team grows to 40 TiB a
month. On-demand is 39 × €5.66, about €221. Now the ratio decides it. The
two ratios below are illustrations, not typical values, which is the whole
point of measuring your own:

- At 60 slot-hours per TiB, the month uses 2,400 slot-hours. On Standard
  that's about €96, before the autoscaler's rounding. Slots win.
- At 200 slot-hours per TiB, it's 8,000 slot-hours, about €318. On-demand
  wins, by a lot.

Same bill today, opposite answers. That's why "switch once you pass X TiB"
advice misleads.

## What about predictability, the other reason people switch?

A reservation with a maximum slot count gives you a hard ceiling per hour.
A Standard reservation capped at 100 slots in the EU can't cost more than
about €4 an hour, whatever anyone runs. On-demand's worst case is one
careless `SELECT *` across your biggest table.

But you don't need to change models to get a ceiling. On-demand has its own
brakes: a maximum bytes billed setting per query, and custom daily quotas
per project or per user. If a surprise bill is what scares you, set those
first. They are free and take ten minutes.

## Standard or Enterprise?

Standard is the cheapest slot, but Google's
[editions comparison](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/editions-intro)
lists it without the fine-grained security controls: column-level access,
row-level security and data masking. It also has no BI Engine. If you protect
personal data columns with policy tags, which is a sensible GDPR habit, your
realistic comparison is Enterprise at €0.060 per slot-hour. In the EU
multi-region that drops the break-even to about 95 slot-hours per TiB.

Commitments are the other Enterprise lever. One-year and three-year
commitments exist only on Enterprise and Enterprise Plus. They start at 50
slots, are tied to one region and renew automatically unless you cancel. In
the EU, a one-year Enterprise commitment is about €0.048 per slot-hour and a
three-year one about €0.036. But a commitment runs around the clock: 50 slots
for a year in the EU comes to roughly €1,744 a month. That's a floor most
five-person teams have no reason to pay.

## So which should you pick?

- **Under a few TiB a month:** stay on-demand, set maximum bytes billed and a
  daily quota, and move on.
- **Tens of TiB a month:** run the query above. Well under the break-even
  for your region and edition, test a reservation on one project with fluid
  scaling on. Near or over it, stay put.
- **Personal data behind column or row security:** compare against
  Enterprise, not Standard.
- **Steady load all day, every day, for years:** that's the only case where
  a commitment earns its floor.
