A pilot is a magic trick done at close range. Someone picks the data, fixes
it by hand, and the audience sees it work. Then you try to run the trick
every day, for everyone, on data nobody picked. That is where most European
AI projects stall, and the numbers are consistent about it.

## How many AI pilots actually reach production?

Fewer than you would hope, though every figure comes from a survey with its
own sample, so treat them as a pattern, not a constant. A July 2026 report
from the consultancy OMMAX surveyed 250 senior decision-makers in Germany,
France, Italy, the Netherlands and the UK. It found that 79% of AI
initiatives fail during or after the pilot stage, and 65% exceed their budget
([Consultancy.eu](https://www.consultancy.eu/news/14088/european-companies-face-ai-execution-gap-as-79-of-initiatives-miss-their-goals)).
A separate vendor survey by Alteryx, run by Coleman Parkes with 525 EU
respondents, reports that around three quarters of pilots fail to reach
production
([IT Europa](https://iteuropa.com/news/eu-firms-struggle-scale-ai-beyond-pilots-says-alteryx-research)).
Alteryx sells data tooling, so weigh its framing accordingly. The direction
still agrees across independent sources.

## What stops them?

Not the model. In the OMMAX survey the biggest barriers to scaling were
integration complexity (40%) and data quality (28%). Alteryx names weak data
foundations and low trust in outputs, and points at a specific habit:
putting generative AI straight on top of raw, poorly governed sources, which
makes inconsistent answers and hallucinations more likely.

Ownership matters too. In the same OMMAX data, IT owns most AI initiatives
(48%) and business units own only 7%. A feature that nobody in the business
is accountable for is a feature nobody notices drifting.

## Why does the demo hide the problem?

Because the pilot quietly includes a data team of one. Look at the diagram.

In the top row, a person picks a small extract, cleans it, and feeds the
feature something tidy. That person is the missing layer. In the bottom row
the same feature meets live sources: duplicates, renamed columns, a field
that was free text last quarter. Nobody is cleaning it, so the answers drift,
people stop trusting them, and use fades. The model did not change. The
cleaning did.

## What is the layer underneath, in plain words?

Three boring things. Data that is checked on its way in, so a broken source
fails loudly instead of feeding the model quietly. Definitions that are
written down once, so "active customer" means the same thing to the feature
and to finance. And an owner, a named person who gets the alert when it
breaks. I wrote about the same gap from the agent side in
[the post on agents and governed data](/work/2026-09-agents-need-governed-data),
and the usual causes of a shaky foundation are in
[the five medallion mistakes](/work/2026-08-medallion-mistakes).

## What is the five-question checklist before you scale a pilot?

1. **Where does production data come from?** Name the live sources. If the
   pilot used an extract, who made it, and how?
2. **What did the person fixing the extract actually fix?** Write the fixes
   down. Each one is a check the pipeline now has to run on its own.
3. **Who owns the answer?** One named person in the business, not only IT.
4. **How will you notice drift?** A small set of questions with known
   answers, run weekly, is enough to start.
5. **What happens when a source breaks?** The right answer is that the feature
   says "I don't know", not that it answers confidently with stale data.

If you can answer all five, scale. If you can answer three, fix the other two
first. That is cheaper than the six months of quiet disappointment between a
great demo and a switched-off feature.
