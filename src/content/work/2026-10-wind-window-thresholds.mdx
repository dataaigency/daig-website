Ask a kitesurfer whether 18 knots is a lot of wind and you will get a
question back: on which kite? On a big 12 metre kite, 18 knots is the top of
the comfortable range. On a 9, it is the sweet spot. On a 7, it is barely
getting started. Same number, three different answers, and nobody on the
beach thinks that is strange.

Now look at the alerts in your company. Most of them are a number and a
line. Revenue below X. Pipeline duration above Y. Error count above Z. When
they fire, the question nobody can answer is the one every kitesurfer asks
first: compared to what, and for whom?

## What is a wind window, really?

A wind window is the range of conditions in which a given setup works. It
has a floor, because below it the kite will not keep you moving. It has a
ceiling, because above it the kite is too much. And the floor and ceiling
depend on the gear you rigged.

Kite size charts make this concrete. One manufacturer's chart for a rider of
70 to 80 kilograms suggests roughly 13 to 15 square metres at 10 to 13
knots, 11 to 12 at 14 to 17, 9 to 10 at 18 to 22, and 7 to 8 at 23 to 28
([Airush](https://airush.com/blogs/news/what-kite-size-should-you-choose-based-on-the-wind)).
Other brands and riders shift those numbers, and the sources are clear that
these are guidelines, not standards. That is the point. The window belongs
to the combination of kite, rider and conditions, not to the wind alone.

The data world has the same idea under a drier name: an operating range. A
pipeline that runs in forty minutes on a Monday and ninety on a month-end
close is not broken. Ninety minutes on a quiet Tuesday might be.

## Why does one threshold fail?

Because a single line cannot tell a normal gust from a real problem.

Wind is not one number. At any moment it swings between lulls and gusts.
Guidance for kiters says to choose your kite for the top gust rather than the
average, and that gusts usually last under 20 seconds, so watching a live
reading for a few minutes tells you more than any one value
([MAC Kiteboarding](https://www.mackiteboarding.com/kitesurfing-safely-in-gusty-conditions.htm)).
A simple alarm at 20 knots would ring on every gust of a perfectly good
afternoon, and stay silent when the average is fine but the swings are
wild.

Your monitoring does the same thing. A threshold on a metric that
naturally swings with the day of the week, the month end or the marketing
campaign fires on harmless moves, and stays quiet during a slow drift that
never crosses the line. After a few weeks the team learns the lesson the
threshold taught: these alerts do not mean anything. Then the one that does
arrives and gets the same shrug.

## What does a window check instead?

Three questions, in order, and the order matters.

**Is it in range for what is running?** The floor and ceiling depend on the
setup. In a data stack that means comparing a job against its own usual range
for this day and this load, not against one number for all days. Static limits
still have a place for hard boundaries like a contractual deadline. Everything
that naturally varies deserves a range that varies with it.

**Is the spread small enough to trust the average?** Kiters talk about the
gap between lulls and gusts. One forum rule of thumb treats up to about 5
knots of variation as normal and more than 8 as risky for beginners
([Seabreeze](https://www.seabreeze.com.au/forums/Kitesurfing/Newbies-Tips-Tricks/What-amount-of-wind-Gust-is-ok)),
though nobody pretends it is an official standard. The data version is
variance. A daily total that is "about right" because a big error and a big
omission cancelled each other out is a gust-heavy day. The average looks fine.
Do not trust it.

**Is the direction safe?** Offshore wind is the one that experienced kiters
treat as the main hazard, because it keeps pushing you away from the coast
and into open water if something fails
([Seabreeze](https://www.seabreeze.com.au/forums/Kitesurfing/Victoria/On-shore-vs-Off-shore-in-Vic-that-is-)).
Direction here is a failure mode, not a size. Some conditions are worse
because of what happens when things go wrong. In a data stack, think of the
feed that quietly serves a finance report versus the feed that only fills a
spare dashboard. The same stall deserves a different response.

Any no, and you stay on shore. Three yeses, and you go. No heroics, no
negotiation with the forecast.

## How does this reduce alert noise?

It moves the question from "is the number big?" to "is this outside normal
for this situation, and does it matter?" That is a shift from alerting on
causes to alerting on symptoms, the idea at the heart of the Google SRE
chapter on monitoring: page a human only for something that is urgent, real
and actionable, and keep the rest as dashboards and tickets
([Google SRE book](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)).
A conference talk drawing on that chapter adds the human cost: people can only
respond with urgency a few times a day before the fatigue sets in
([Rabenstein, SREcon 2016](https://www.usenix.net/sites/default/files/conference/protected-files/srecon16europe_slides_rabenstein.pdf)).
I have not found a trustworthy number for how many alerts teams ignore, and
the figures that circulate come from vendor blogs with no method behind them,
so I will not quote one. Ask your own team instead. Which alerts do people
mute without reading? That list is your noise budget.

For a small team the practical version is short:

1. List the alerts that fired last month. Mark each as acted on, ignored or muted.
2. For every ignored one, ask what the window should have been: what range is normal for this day and this load?
3. Add the spread and the stakes. Alert on gusty behaviour and on who downstream depends on the number.
4. Keep a few hard lines for deadlines and compliance. Not everything needs a window.

## Where does the metaphor stop working?

The wind has a physical cliff. Past a certain strength, no amount of skill
makes a small kite a good idea, and the sea punishes a bad call within
minutes. Most business metrics have no such cliff. Your normal range drifts
as the company grows, and the right window last year is the wrong one now.
So a data window needs reviewing on a schedule, which the beach never asks
of you. And a kiter can see the water and feel the pull. A pipeline gives no
such feedback until someone has built it, which is why the checks have to be
written down.
