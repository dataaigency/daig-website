"Is Power BI GDPR compliant?" is a bit like asking whether a kitchen is
hygienic. The kitchen can be excellent. What matters is what you do in it.

So the honest answer is: Power BI can be used in a GDPR-compliant way, and
whether yours is depends on settings and habits that are yours, not
Microsoft's. Here is the split.

## What does Microsoft cover?

Under GDPR you are the controller of your customers' and staff's personal data.
Microsoft is your processor. The law says a controller may only use processors
that give sufficient guarantees, and Microsoft presents its data protection
addendum as exactly that for its commercial customers. The GDPR terms sit as an
attachment to it and commit Microsoft to the processor duties in Article 28
([Microsoft's GDPR commitments](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/legal/gdpr)).

Be aware that this is not universally accepted. In 2022 the German Data
Protection Conference concluded the addendum did not meet the requirements of
Article 28(3), and in 2023 seven German supervisory authorities published
guidance for controllers on using it
([ReuschLaw's summary](https://www.reuschlaw.de/en/news/guidelines-for-microsoft-365/)).
That debate is about Microsoft 365 as a whole, not Power BI alone. It does mean
a careful German or Dutch customer should read the current terms, not just
trust a badge.

So the contract exists. It is the starting line, not the finish.

## Where does Power BI keep my data?

In the region your tenant lives in. Microsoft also runs an EU Data Boundary, a
commitment to store and process customer data for its enterprise services inside
the EU, with documented exceptions. Microsoft is explicit that this is a
contractual commitment, not a legal jurisdiction
([Microsoft Power Platform docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-platform/admin/macro-regions)).

Two wrinkles worth knowing. If you need workspaces placed in a specific other
region, that is Multi-Geo, which is a capacity feature, and the capacity's
region cannot be changed after creation; you create a new one and move the
workspaces
([Multi-Geo for Power BI Embedded](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/Power-Bi/developer/embedded/embedded-multi-geo)).
Microsoft's Microsoft 365 documentation also says tenants that bought Multi-Geo
fall outside the EU Data Boundary scope, so check which commitment you actually
want before you buy it
([EU Data Boundary for Microsoft 365](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/enterprise/m365-dr-eu-data-boundary?view=o365-worldwide)).

Residency is also only half of sovereignty. Where the bytes sit does not decide
whose law can reach them, which I explained in
[the EU region post](/work/2026-09-eu-region-not-sovereignty).

## What about Copilot and the AI features?

Check the tenant setting. Microsoft's Power BI blog said in 2024 that capacities
in the EU no longer need the cross-geography switch to use Copilot, with
traffic staying inside the EU data boundary
([Power BI blog](https://powerbi.microsoft.com/en-gb/blog/copilot-in-power-bi-soon-available-to-more-users-in-your-organization/)).
The setting that allows processing outside your geography applies to customers
whose capacity sits outside the EU and US
([Fabric admin portal docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/fabric/admin/service-admin-portal-copilot)).
I could not reach Microsoft's pages directly while writing this, so I am going
off those summaries. Open the admin portal, find the Copilot section, and read
what your tenant says today.

## Which settings decide whether personal data stays controlled?

Three, and they are all yours.

**Publish to web.** This creates a public link that anyone can open with no
sign-in, and there is no built-in audit of who looked. It lives in the tenant
settings and can be off for everyone or limited to named groups. US government
baselines say disable it unless the mission needs it
([CISA's Power BI baseline](https://cisa.gov/resources-tools/services/m365-power-bi)).
A report with customer names behind a public link is a data breach waiting for
someone to forward the URL.

**Export.** Every export puts a copy of the data into a file, and a file is out
of reach of everything Power BI does for you. Decide who may export, and let
that be a group rather than "everyone".

**Who sees which rows.** Named seats plus row-level security means the sales
lead in Ghent sees Ghent. That is both better GDPR practice (data minimisation)
and a better dashboard.

None of these three are a Microsoft failing. They are the part of the handshake
the processor cannot do for you, because only you know who should see what.

## What does a sane Power BI GDPR setup look like?

- The Microsoft data protection addendum is accepted, and you know where your
  tenant's region is.
- Publish to web is off, or limited to a short list of people.
- Export is limited to a named group.
- Reports containing personal data use row-level security and named seats.
- Personal data is minimised upstream: if a dashboard needs a region, do not
  load the customer's address.
- Your records of processing mention Power BI, and you have decided whether the
  AI features are on.

Notice what is not on the list: a certificate. Certifications describe how a
vendor runs its side. Your side is configuration plus a decision per report.
If you are weighing the wider platform, the same logic applies to Fabric; I walk

I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. For anything high stakes, your
data protection officer or counsel has the final word.
