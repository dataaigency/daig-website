"We set BigQuery to EU" is a sentence I hear a lot. It is usually true. It is
also usually less precise than the person saying it thinks, and one part of it
cannot be fixed later without moving all your data. That is worth knowing
before the first table exists, not after the hundredth.

This post is the practical half of a story I started in
[an EU region is not sovereignty](/work/2026-09-eu-region-not-sovereignty).
That one asked who can be compelled to hand data over. This one asks a simpler
question first: where do the bytes actually sit, and what did you commit to
when you picked?

## What does the BigQuery "EU" location actually mean?

It means a multi-region, and your data lives in just one of two places inside
it. Google's documentation says data in the EU multi-region is only stored in
europe-west1 (Belgium) or europe-west4 (the Netherlands), and that BigQuery
decides which one automatically
([BigQuery locations](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/locations)).
You do not get to choose. You also do not get Frankfurt, Paris or Madrid
from that setting, even though all three are EU cities.

For most companies that is fine. "Inside the EU, in one of two EU countries"
satisfies the usual contract line. It stops being fine when a client or a
sector regulator names a country, or when your own policy says Germany. Then
the multi-region is the wrong tool and a single region is the right one.

## Which single regions can I choose instead?

BigQuery offers regions across the EU, including europe-west1 (Belgium),
europe-west4 (Netherlands), europe-west3 (Frankfurt), europe-west9 (Paris)
and europe-southwest1 (Madrid)
([BigQuery locations](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/locations)).
The list changes as Google opens regions, so check the page on the day you
decide rather than trusting a blog post, this one included.

A single region gives you control over the country. It costs you something
too: the multi-region is not a safety net, and a single region is not either.
Google is explicit that choosing a multi-region does not give you cross-region
replication or protection from a regional outage
([cross-region dataset replication](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/data-replication)).
Your data sits in one region inside it. If you want a second copy somewhere
else, that is a feature you turn on, with a read-only secondary replica in a
region you name.

## Can I change the location later?

No. After you create a dataset, its location cannot be changed
([BigQuery locations](https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/locations)). The
usual fix is to create a new dataset in the right place and copy the data
across. For a small team that is a weekend of work. For a platform with
dozens of datasets, scheduled queries, views and dashboards pointing at them,
it is a project with a name and a budget.

That is the real reason to decide early. Residency is not hard. Changing your
mind about residency is.

## What breaks when my datasets sit in different locations?

Queries stop working across the gap. A query job runs in one location, and
datasets in other locations are not visible to it. The classic symptom is the
error that says a dataset was "not found in location"
([a typical write-up of the error](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-08-27-fix-bigquery-dataset-not-found-location-us/markdown)),
when the dataset plainly exists. It exists. It is just somewhere else.

This bites in three ordinary ways. A team creates its first dataset in the
default US location by accident, then adds an EU one and wonders why the join
fails. An export lands in a Cloud Storage bucket in one region while the
dataset reading it sits in another. Or two projects, set up by two different
people, quietly pick two different locations.

Two settings help. You can pin a project default with an
`ALTER PROJECT` statement that sets `default_location`, so new datasets stop
landing in the wrong place, and you can set the location explicitly on
automated jobs instead of letting BigQuery guess. Google has also been
previewing "global queries", which can reference data across locations, but
you have to enable them and they copy remote data to a primary location, so
read the residency consequences before you switch that on.

## What does the EU setting still not cover?

Three things, and they are the honest limits of "we picked the EU".

First, a location is about storage and processing, not about who the provider
answers to. A US-headquartered provider running an EU region is still a
US-headquartered provider, which is the whole subject of
[the sovereignty post](/work/2026-09-eu-region-not-sovereignty).

Second, the location of a dataset says nothing about where you send data
afterwards. A dashboard tool, an export to a spreadsheet, an AI service
called from a query: each is its own transfer, with its own location, and
each needs its own answer. If you use BigQuery next to AI services, check the
region of each one separately. I walk through that on the AI side in
[the EU-hosted LLM post](/work/2026-09-eu-hosted-llm-menu).

Third, GDPR itself does not demand EU-only storage. It demands a lawful basis
for transfers outside the EEA. Residency requirements usually come from a
contract, a sector rule or company policy. Find out which of those you are
actually answering to before you pay for stricter than you need.

## So which location should I pick?

Here is the short version I give small teams.

- **Nobody has named a country.** Use the EU multi-region. It is simple, and
  "stored in Belgium or the Netherlands" is a defensible sentence.
- **A contract, regulator or policy names a country.** Use that single region,
  from day one, in every environment.
- **Everything sits in one place already.** Set the project default location
  now so the next dataset does not drift.
- **Some data is already in the wrong place.** Move it while the platform is
  small. It only gets harder.

Then write the decision down in one line, in the README of the repo that
creates your datasets. Future you will want to know it was a choice and not
an accident.
