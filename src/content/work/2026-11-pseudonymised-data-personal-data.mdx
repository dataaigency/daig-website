Most data teams have done this at least once. Swap the customer
name for a token, hash the email column, send the table to an agency or a
partner and call it "anonymised". It feels like the responsible thing to
do. It is a good thing to do. It just does not mean what most people think
it means, and the law around it is moving right now.

On 21 October 2026, EU member states agreed their negotiating position on
the Digital Omnibus, the package that reopens parts of the GDPR. One of the
changes is a new Article 25a on pseudonymised data
([Addleshaw Goddard](https://www.addleshawgoddard.com/en/insights/insights-briefings/2026/data-protection/eu-digital-omnibus-european-council-adopts-its-position/),
[Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/11/09/digital-rulebook-simplification-of-eu-data-protection-and-cyber-rules-102o7e4)).
The headlines read as if pseudonymised data is about to fall outside the
GDPR. For most small and mid-sized companies, it is not. Here is what
actually changes, for whom, and what to do while it is still being argued.

## What is pseudonymised data, legally?

The GDPR defines pseudonymisation as processing personal data so it can no
longer be tied to a person without extra information, as long as that extra
information (the key, the lookup table, the salt) is kept separately and
protected ([GDPR Art. 4(5)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-4-gdpr/)). Recital 26
then says the quiet part: data that could be re-attributed using that extra
information is still personal data
([Recital 26](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-26/)).

Anonymised data is the other thing. It cannot be tied back to anyone, by
anyone, with means reasonably likely to be used. Only that falls outside the
GDPR. A hashed email is pseudonymised, not anonymised: anyone holding the
same email list can hash it the same way and match the rows.

## Who is it personal data for?

This is where it got interesting in September 2025. In *EDPS v SRB*
(C-413/23 P), the Court of Justice looked at pseudonymised comments that the
EU's Single Resolution Board had sent to Deloitte. The Court said the answer
depends on who is looking
([FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/rethinking-personal-data-the-cjeus-contextual-turn-in-edps-vs-srb/),
[Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/09/analysis-of-the-cjeu-judgment)).

For the SRB, which held the key, the comments were personal data, full stop.
For Deloitte, which had no key and no realistic way to get one, they might
not be. But the Court also held that the SRB still owed the people involved
the usual transparency about who would receive their data, judged from the
SRB's side at the time it collected it. What the data is for the recipient
does not change what you owe as the sender.

## What would the new Article 25a actually say?

The Commission's November 2025 proposal wanted to rewrite the definition of
personal data itself, in Article 4(1), to build that "depends on who is
looking" idea into the core of the GDPR
([White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/gdpr-under-revision-key-takeaways-from-digital-omnibus-regulation-proposal)).
The EDPB and the EDPS objected in a joint opinion in February 2026, and
member states have now dropped that rewrite
([LEXIA](https://www.lexia.it/en/2026/03/12/digital-omnibus-edpb-edps/)).

In its place, the Council's mandate (document ST 14756/26) adds a narrower
rule on pseudonymised data alone. As reported by the firms that have read
it, it does four things:

- **A recipient that cannot identify anyone is outside it.** Pseudonymised
  data is not personal data for a third party with no means to re-identify.
- **Passing it on can switch it back on.** If that third party hands the
  data to someone who can identify people, the handover and that recipient's
  use are processing of personal data again.
- **Processors get no exemption.** For a processor working on your behalf,
  pseudonymised data stays personal data, always.
- **The EDPB, not the Commission, writes the detail.** The Commission's
  planned implementing acts on re-identification are replaced by an EDPB
  opinion on pseudonymisation and anonymisation.

Notice what is missing from that list: you. Nothing in it changes the status
of the data for the company that pseudonymised it and still holds the key.

## Is any of this law yet?

No. The Council position is one side of a negotiation. The European
Parliament handles the file in a joint committee of its industry and civil
liberties committees, and as of late October it had not voted. Several
trackers expect a committee vote in the first quarter of 2027 at the
earliest, after which the two sides still have to agree a final text
([Praxikon](https://www.praxikon.com/en/avg/digital-omnibus/ep-ontwerpverslag)).
Until then the GDPR applies exactly as written, and the Parliament may want
something quite different.

Meanwhile the EDPB has been busy on the other side of the line. Its draft
Guidelines 02/2026 on anonymisation, open for consultation until 30 October
2026, set a three-part test (no singling out a record, no linking, no
inferring) and treat anonymising itself as processing that needs a legal
basis
([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_202602_anonymisation_v1_en_0.pdf)).
In plain words: "we anonymised it" is a claim you will be asked to prove.

## What does this mean for a small data team?

Look at who actually receives your pseudonymised tables. In most small companies,
it is the BI agency, the analytics consultant, the cloud data
platform, the marketing tool. Those are processors working under a data
processing agreement. Under the Council's own text, nothing changes for
them. The data is personal data, the agreement still matters, and so does
everything else.

Where the new rule could genuinely help is real data sharing with
independent parties: a research partner, a benchmarking pool, an industry
body that only ever sees tokens. If the rule lands as drafted, and if the
recipient truly cannot re-identify, that recipient may not carry GDPR duties
for that data. You still do.

## What should you do now, before the law settles?

None of these depend on how the negotiation ends:

1. **Keep the key somewhere else.** The lookup table or salt lives in a
   separate workspace or project, with its own access list, not in the same
   schema as the tokens. In a lakehouse, that usually means pseudonymising
   on the way into silver and locking the mapping away from everyone who
   only needs the analytics.
2. **Name things honestly.** Stop calling hashed tables "anonymised" in
   documentation, contracts and dashboards. The word has a legal meaning and
   a regulator will read it that way.
3. **Sort recipients into two lists.** Processors on one, independent third
   parties on the other. Only the second list is even in scope for the
   proposed change.
4. **Write down why a recipient cannot re-identify.** What do they hold that
   could be joined to your tokens? If you cannot answer that today, the new
   rule would not help you tomorrow.
